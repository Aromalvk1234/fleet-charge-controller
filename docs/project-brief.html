Fleet Charge Controller

A scheduler and edge-control system that charges a 50-truck electric depot through 11 shared bays. Each truck gets exactly the energy its trip needs, at the gentlest and cheapest rate that still gets it to the gate before dispatch.

This brief documents the whole project: the problem, the models, the math, the scheduler, the simulator, the language-model layer, the measured results, and what is still missing. Every number in the charts and tables comes from the project's own simulator, run from the code in the repository. Figures marked synthetic use generated prices, solar, arrivals and trips.

How to read the resultsCosts are for one simulated day of a 50-truck depot under a made-up but plausible tariff. They show how the policies rank against each other. They are not a forecast of any real site's bill.
1 · Requirements

The problem

A depot operator has trucks that return at different times, must leave at different times, and need different amounts of energy for the next trip. Electricity costs change through the day. Solar panels give free energy at midday. Chargers are scarce: there are fewer bays than trucks, and a Ford E-Transit cannot use a bay built for a Mercedes eActros. Fast charging is cheap in time and bad for the battery.

The controller has to answer four questions continuously:

  1. How much energy does each truck need? It depends on distance, cargo weight, terrain, temperature, the battery's age, and a safety buffer.
  2. Which truck takes which bay, and when? Trucks wait in a lot for a bay of their own model.
  3. How fast should each truck charge? Slowly when there is slack, so it costs less and wears the battery less. Quickly when the deadline is close.
  4. Is everyone ready at dispatch time, with the connector off, the truck at the gate and a margin to spare?

2 · Inputs

Fleet and site

The fleet uses three real truck models. Battery, range and charging figures come from public manufacturer and press sources (checked October 2026). Mass, drag and drivetrain values are engineering estimates for the energy model, and the model is then calibrated to the published range (§4).

The site

  • Grid import limit 1,500 kW; solar peak 900 kW; charger efficiency 93 % (grid kWh to battery kWh).
  • Price: a time-of-use base (0.09 $/kWh overnight, 0.14 daytime, 0.32 from 16:00 to 21:00) with hourly noise and one real-time spike of 1.8x in the evening peak.
  • Demand charge: 0.55 $ per kW of the day's peak grid import, a prorated monthly tariff.
  • The day runs from 12:00 to 12:00 in 96 slots of 15 minutes. Arrivals are 12:30–22:30 (peak about 17:30); departures are 04:30–12:00 next day. About 18 % of vans make a same-day short turn.

The fleet's spread

Every truck has its own random arrival SOC (15–45 %), its own true SOH (82–100 %), cargo between 15 % and 100 % of the model's maximum payload, terrain between 1 and 8 m of climb per km, and a priority (15 % of trucks count double when they miss a target). If a generated trip would need more than 95 % of the battery, the route is shortened by 4 % steps until it fits, as a dispatcher would.

3 · Structure

Architecture

The design rule: language models read and explain, deterministic optimisation decides. Deadlines must hold, so no model sets a power level or picks a bay. The system works on two time scales: a slow planning loop over 15-minute slots and a fast edge loop every minute.

Why this fits the Best Apps and Agents track

It is an app a depot manager would actually use: a live yard view, a schedule, a policy comparison and a dispatcher panel. Nemotron on Nebius Token Factory handles the language work (parsing dispatcher notes, reading live temperature found by Tavily, explaining decisions), while a deterministic optimiser makes the charging decisions so every plan stays safe and checkable.

4 · How much energy

Trip energy model

A flat "kWh per km" ignores the things a dispatcher knows matter: a loaded tractor-trailer burns a lot more than an empty one, a hilly route costs more than a flat one, and cold weather costs a lot on every truck. The model builds the trip's battery energy from physics and then scales it once per truck model so that, at reference conditions, it reproduces the consumption implied by the published range.

Forces and losses

m = mcurb + mpayloadmass
Eroll = Crr · m · g · drolling resistance
Eaero = ½ · ρ · CdA · (1.05 v̄)² · daerodynamic drag
Eprop = (Eroll + Eaero) / ηbattery to wheel
κ = 1/η − η · rnet cost of one joule of motion energy, with regeneration fraction r
Estop = ns · ½ m vs² · κ, ns = (stops per km) · dstop-and-go
Eclimb = m · g · h · κ, h = (ascent per km) · dclimbing, net of descent
Phvac = kh · max(0, 18 − T) + 0.6 kh · max(0, T − 24)heating and cooling, T in °C
Eaux = (Paux + Phvac) · d / v̄auxiliaries over the trip time
fcold = 1 + 0.004 · max(0, 15 − T)cold battery and tyres
Etrip = c · fcold · (Eprop + Estop + Eclimb) + Eauxbattery energy for the round trip

d is the round-trip distance, v̄ the average speed, g = 9.81 m/s², ρ = 1.2 kg/m³. Energies in joules convert to kWh by dividing by 3.6 million.

Calibration

The constant c is solved per model so that at reference conditions (100 km, half the maximum payload, 3 m/km ascent, 15 °C) the model returns the consumption derived from the published range, ĉ = usable kWh ÷ rated range:

c = (ĉ · dref − Eaux,ref) / (Eprop,ref + Estop,ref + Eclimb,ref)

The fitted values are . A value near 1 means the physics alone already matches the published range. The van's higher value reflects that its published range is an EPA figure and the 0.45 kWh/km used here is deliberately conservative.

Required energy at departure

Cplan = Cnom · SOHrepusable capacity as the controller sees it
Ereq = Etrip · (1 + μ) + ρres · Cplanμ = 15 % margin, ρres = 10 % reserve
target SOC = Ereq / Cplancapped at 95 % by shortening the route

The margin covers model error, detours and driving style. The reserve means the truck never gets home empty. Both are policy settings. An older battery has a smaller Cplan, so the same trip needs a higher target SOC.

Sensitivity

Consumption in kWh per km for each model as one input changes, others held at reference. Rows come straight from the model.

5 · What the trucks tell the yard

SOC and SOH telemetry

The controller never reads a truck's true battery. Each truck reports its state of charge every slot and its state of health once. Both reports are noisy. The physics runs on the true values; the scheduler plans on the reported ones.

SOCrep = clip( SOC + 𝒩(0, σs²), 0, 1 )σs = 1 % absolute
SOHrep = SOH + 𝒩(0, σh²)σh = 1 %, true SOH spread 82–100 %
Pmax(SOH) = Prated · (1 − 0.5 · (1 − SOH))older packs accept less power
Ctrue = Cnom · SOHphysical capacity

A 1 % SOC error is 0.9 kWh on a van and 5.9 kWh on an eActros. This is why the planner aims slightly above the target (a 2 % cushion) and why the edge agent checks the true energy every minute and adds power if reality falls behind (§10).

6 · Charging

Charging physics

Charging adds energy at the charger's efficiency, limited by the charger rating, the truck's SOH-derated acceptance, and the way batteries slow down near full.

et+1 = et + ηc · pt · Δtηc = 0.93, Δt = 15 min in the planner, 1 min in the simulator
pt ≤ Pmax · ( φ + ct · (1 − SOCt) ), ct = (1 − φ) / (1 − s0)taper above s0 = 80 %, floor φ = 30 % of Pmax
pt + (Pmax ct / C) · et ≤ Pmax (φ + ct)same bound, linear in e and p, so it fits the LP

In the simulator a thermal state also matters: battery temperature follows the charge rate with a 20-minute time constant, and above 45 °C the edge agent halves the power.

7 · Battery care

Battery health

A validated ageing model is out of scope for a simulated project. The planner uses a transparent proxy that keeps the problem solvable and captures the two things operators do to protect packs: charge gently and do not sit full.

Jrate = Σt Σk ρk · pk,t · Δt, pt = Σk pk,tconvex tiers
tier bounds (C-rate): 0.15, 0.35, 1.0 · cost ρk ($/kWh): 0.002, 0.010, 0.035
Jdwell = λh · Σt max(0, et − 0.8 C) · Δtλh = 0.004 $ per kWh above 80 % per hour

Because the tiers are convex, an LP fills the cheap tier first without any integer variables. The dwell term pushes charging towards the end of the window so trucks do not wait fully charged for hours. Both terms are small next to energy cost; their job is to break ties in favour of the battery.

8 · Stage 2 of the scheduler

Power planner (LP)

Given a charging window for each truck, the planner chooses the charge power in every slot. Variables: pv,t (grid-side kW, split across the three rate tiers), ev,t (battery kWh), hv,t (kWh above 80 %), sv (missed energy), gt (grid import) and Ppk (peak import).

min Σt πt gt Δt + λd Ppk + Σv(Jrate + Jdwell)v + Σv M ωv svenergy + demand + health + missed energy
ev,t+1 = ev,t + ηc pv,t Δtenergy balance
ev,0 = reported energy now; ev,n + sv ≥ Ereq,v + 0.02 Cplan,vreach the target, with a 2 % cushion
0 ≤ pv,t ≤ Pmax,v(SOH) and taper bound (§6); pv,t = 0 outside the truck's window
Σv pv,t − gt ≤ solart, gt ≤ Ppk ≤ grid limitsite balance and peak

πt is the price, λd the demand-charge rate, ωv the truck's priority weight and M = 50 $ per missed kWh. The slack sv keeps the LP feasible when a deadline cannot be met; the penalty makes missing the deadline the last thing the optimiser accepts, and the shortfall is reported to the operator.

Why the demand charge mattersWith the demand charge, the planner spreads load and holds site peak to a few hundred kW. Without it, an experiment on the unconstrained model charged more kWh in the cheapest hours (47 % against 24 %) but hit the 1,500 kW grid limit, and the demand charge on that peak cost more than the energy it saved.
9 · The scheduler

Bay scheduler

With 50 trucks and 11 bays, charging power is not the only decision. Someone has to decide who gets a bay and for how long. The pools are separate: the 6 van bays only take Ford E-Transits, the 3 regional bays only take Volvos, the 2 long-haul bays only take Mercedes trucks.

Anatomy of a bay visit

Stage 1: sequencing with a time-indexed MILP

Each pool is solved separately. A truck that still needs charge is a job with a release slot rj (arrival, or now), a deadline Dj (departure minus one buffer slot) and a duration that depends on its own battery:

kj = min { k : ek ≥ Etarget }, ei+1 = ei + ηc · min(Pmax(SOH), taper(ei)) · Δtflat-out slots, with taper
pj = 1 + kj + 1plug-in + charge + unplug

Binary xj,t = 1 when job j starts at slot t.

min Σj,t cj,t xj,t
Σt xj,t = 1 for every job jevery truck starts exactly once
Σj Στ=t−pj+1…t xj,τ + ft ≤ nb for every slot tat most nb bays busy; ft = trucks already docked
cj,t = w ωj (t − rj) + π̄j,t Ej + M ωj Elate,j,twaiting + energy price + lateness

Here w = 0.5 $ per slot of queueing, π̄j,t is the average price over the slots the truck would charge in, Ej its energy, and Elate the energy it could not receive if t + pj overruns the deadline. Capacity constraints have the consecutive-ones structure of interval scheduling, so the model is small and solves fast (about 0.4 s for all three pools). Trucks already in a bay are fixed jobs. If the solver returns nothing, an earliest-deadline-first rule is used.

Stage 1b: widening

Stage 1 books the shortest possible visit. That leaves no room for slow, cheap charging. Stage 1b grows each window, earliest deadline first, into any bay-slots that no one else has booked, in both directions. A truck in a quiet pool might get hours of slack and charge gently on solar power; a truck in a crowded pool gets only what it needs.

Stage 2: power inside the windows

The LP of §8 runs with each truck's window as its allowed charging slots: from the slot after the plug-in slot to the slot before the unplug slot. Sequencing and power are therefore chosen in two steps that each see the other's output: stage 1 sees prices and deadlines, stage 2 sees solar, the site cap, the demand charge and battery health.

When it re-plans

Plans are rebuilt on arrivals, on departures of trucks that were not ready, when a truck finishes and frees a bay, when a charger fails or comes back, and at least every four slots. Each re-plan starts from the trucks' reported energy, so the plan always reflects the latest telemetry.

10 · Digital twin

Yard simulator

The simulator is the stand-in for the real depot. It owns the true battery state of every truck, runs the physics each minute, and enforces bays and handling delays.

The edge agent

Every minute each charging truck's agent computes the power it will actually draw:

p = min( pplan + pguard, taper(SOC), Pmax(SOH) ) · ssite · (0.5 if Tbatt > 45 °C)
pguard = max(0, Ereq + 0.004 C − etrue − ηc Σfuture pplan Δt) / (ηc · time to ready-by)only when behind plan
ssite = min(1, (grid limit + solaractual) / Σ requested)proportional site cap

The guard is zero while the truck is on track, so it never overrides the plan's decision to wait for cheaper power. It only acts when true energy plus the energy still planned would fall short of the requirement.

Costs counted

Energy cost is price times grid import (load minus realised solar, never below zero). Demand cost is the demand rate times the day's highest 15-minute average import. Battery cost applies the tier and dwell terms of §7 to the true charge rates and SOC. Peak, solar use, waiting time, bay utilisation, overcharge (energy beyond the requirement) and on-time rate are logged per run.

What the live calls taught us

  • The Nano model parsed a three-sentence dispatcher note into the right three actions in 7.5 s. Super answered an explanation in 3.7 s.
  • These models spend tokens on reasoning before answering. With a 500-token limit an explanation was cut mid-sentence. The client now allows 1,500 to 2,500 tokens and treats finish_reason: length as a failure to be handled instead of returning half an answer.
  • When Tavily returned no forecast, an early prompt made the model answer 0 °C, which silently inflated every truck's energy by 16 %. The prompt now allows null, and the code validates the range.
  • The model's list endpoint spells the Nano ID with different capitalisation from the one found in search results. Always read the list.
12 · Measured

Results

Eight simulated depot-days (seeds 0–7), four policies each, with realistic delays and noisy telemetry. All trucks in a run share the same random day across policies, so the comparison is paired.

Battery stressThe last three rows are why the smart scheduler is gentler on the packs, not only cheaper. It charges at a lower average rate and leaves trucks sitting full for far fewer hours, because it finishes charging close to departure instead of on arrival.

Reading the tableThe smart scheduler makes trucks wait longer (about 2.7 h on average against 1.2 h) because it holds them until the charging window is cheap. Waiting in the lot costs nothing as long as the truck is ready by dispatch. Bay utilisation rises from 40 % to 67 % because trucks charge slowly while they hold a bay.

One day in detail (seed 0)

Stress: cold weather with charger faults

Six days at −10 °C, with each charging truck facing a 0.4 % chance per slot of its connector failing for two hours.

What does not work yet

  • Occasional misses. The smart scheduler made every departure on all 8 normal days and missed 0.5 trucks per day in the stress runs. The stress misses follow connector faults that remove hours of charging from a truck with a tight deadline. A fault-aware priority bump and earlier re-planning are the next fixes.
  • The ideal bound is not immune. It had no misses on the 8 normal days but 0.17 per day in the stress runs. It plans to the wire, and noise in the SOC reports can leave a truck a few kWh short. That is the effect the planning cushion exists for.
  • Solar use is modest for every policy (31 % for the smart scheduler, 50 % even for the ideal bound) because most trucks arrive after the sun is going down. Using more solar needs daytime dwell time, which this fleet does not have.
13 · Interfaces

Interfaces

Web application

python server.py serves the interface at localhost:8000 and runs the real scheduler and simulator behind it. It simulates all four policies for a chosen seed, temperature and fault rate, then shows:

  • The yard. Bays with trucks whose trailers fill as the battery charges, a time-to-target line on each card (for example "1h 15m to 85%"), per-pool queues with slack chips, and a ready lane. Filters highlight the trucks at risk of missing departure or above 80 % charge and dim the rest.
  • Time navigation. A scrubber marked with the peak-tariff window, solar peak, arrival wave and departure wave, with one-click jumps to each, to the current time of day, and play at 1x, 2x or 4x.
  • Site power. Solar, charging load and grid import against the grid limit on the left axis and price on a right-hand axis. The headroom between grid import and the limit is shaded wherever import comes within 30 % of the limit, and the day's peak is labelled as the value that sets the demand charge.
  • Bay schedule. Who holds which bay and when. Hovering a bar shows arrival charge, target, power and deadline. A cursor follows the scrubber across the schedule, the queue charts and the site power chart.
  • Policy comparison, including a battery-stress panel: average charge rate, truck-hours above 80 % charge, share of energy delivered above 0.15 C, and the wear cost.
  • Truck drawer with the energy breakdown, charge curve, timeline and a Nemotron explanation.
  • Dispatcher drawer with recommended actions, quick scenarios and free-text notes (next section).

The layout adapts from wide desktop monitors down to 1,024 px tablets: grids reflow, the queue and ready lane move under the bays, and nothing scrolls sideways.

Yard design

The yard view follows a design prototype: each charging bay holds a truck drawn side-on whose trailer fills as the battery charges; a dashed line marks the charge the trip needs; amber means charging, green means ready, slate means waiting. Each pool has a queue with a position, wait time and a slack chip (plenty, tight, at risk), and a ready lane. The application renders it from the scheduler's real output, including the plug-in and unplug states.

Dispatcher console

The dispatcher drawer has three parts.

  • Recommended actions. When a truck misses its departure, cannot be charged for its trip, or a charger fails, the drawer proposes concrete fixes with numbers computed from the same models: a delay long enough to finish the charge, the longest route its delivered energy supports, a cargo weight that fits the battery, or marking a faulted charger repaired. One click applies the change and re-plans. For a charger fault it also names which waiting truck to dock in the held bay.
  • Quick scenarios. Grid curtailment (import limit cut to 900 kW), a cold snap (−10 °C), a 3-hour route extension for the regional tractors (held to what each battery can carry), and a reset.
  • Free-text notes. "Truck 5 is carrying 1.6 tonnes of cargo. Truck 30 route is now 280 km. Truck 7 delayed 2 hours." become actions; the energy model recomputes each truck's requirement; the scheduler re-plans. Trips that exceed the battery are flagged immediately.
14 · Quality

Testing

Twelve automated tests run in about 30 seconds (python -m pytest -q):

  • The fleet has 50 trucks of all three models.
  • The energy model reproduces each model's rated consumption at reference conditions.
  • Energy rises with distance, cargo and terrain and cold weather, and falls with lighter cargo.
  • The requirement is trip energy plus the margin plus the reserve, exactly.
  • The unconstrained LP meets every deadline within the site limit.
  • Stage 1 windows never exceed a pool's bay count and always sit between arrival and the dispatch buffer.
  • The full scheduler delivers the energy and never charges in a plug-in slot or after a window.
  • An impossible deadline is reported as a shortfall, not hidden.
  • The dispatcher parser handles delays, route changes, cargo weights and faults.
  • An end-to-end day: everyone dispatched, handling delays present, costs ranked ideal < smart < first-come, and no pool above its bay count at any minute.
15 · Honest limits

Limits and assumptions

  • Everything is simulated. Prices, solar, arrivals, trips, cargo and SOH are generated. Truck battery and charging figures are public; mass, drag and drivetrain parameters are estimates, tied to the real range by calibration.
  • The energy model is physics-flavoured, not a vehicle model. It has no route-level speed profile, wind, traffic or tyre data. The 15 % margin exists because of this.
  • Battery health is a proxy. The tiers and dwell cost are plausible and convex, not fitted to cell ageing data. SOH only changes capacity and power limit; it does not evolve during the day.
  • Telemetry noise is Gaussian and unbiased. Real BMS estimates can be biased, delayed or missing.
  • No uncertainty in the plan. The planner is deterministic with a fixed cushion. A scenario-based or robust planner could use less margin.
  • Bay assignment is per-pool and not preemptive. A truck in a bay stays until it is done. Moving a half-charged truck out for an urgent one is not modelled.
  • No vehicle-to-grid, no site battery, no export. Surplus solar is curtailed.
  • No real charger protocol. The edge agents are simulated; a real deployment would use OCPP or the vendor's interface.
  • Handling delays are drawn from uniform ranges chosen to be plausible, not measured.
